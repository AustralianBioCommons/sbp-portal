What does this mean?

The predicted Template Modelling (pTM) score is a measure of predicted confidence in the overall structure calculated over the entire prediction. As a general rule of thumb:

  • pTM > 0.5: The overall structure is likely correct.
  • pTM < 0.5: The overall structure may be unreliably predicted.

ipTM = 0.91

What does this mean?

The interface predicted Template Modelling (ipTM) score is a measure of the predicted confidence in the relative positions of the subunits forming a complex calculated over the entire prediction. Based on the EBI AlphaFold2 guide (opens in a new tab):

  • ipTM > 0.8: Highly confident, reliable predictions.
  • ipTM < 0.6: Less confident predicted complex.

Note: Confidence scores are intrinsic to the prediction model and cannot be directly compared across different methods.

What am I looking at?

The 3D viewer shows the predicted arrangement of the predicted structure in three-dimensional space. Individual residues are coloured by predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) score. The score ranges from 0 to 100 and reflects the confidence in the local geometry of each residue. Confidence levels often fluctuate across a single protein chain. A detailed module for understanding pLDDT is available in the EBI AlphaFold2 guide (opens in a new tab).

What is PAE?

Predicted Aligned Error (PAE) indicates how confident the model is about the relative position of two residues in the predicted structure. PAE represents the expected error in Ångströms (Å) for the scored position if the predicted structure was aligned to the hypothetical ground truth based on residues at the aligned position. Lower PAE (dark colour) means lower predicted error (higher confidence).

How to read this plot

  • Dark green diagonal line: This line runs from top-left to bottom-right and represents residues aligned against themselves; confidence is always high by definition.
  • Darker/low-error regions: The model is highly confident in the relative 3D position of these residues. When a dark block appears off the main diagonal, it means two distant parts of the protein sequence are confidently folded together into a rigid structure.
  • Lighter/high-error regions: The model is uncertain about their relative position. This often happens when two well-folded domains (which might have great pLDDT scores) are connected by a flexible linker. The modelling tool knows what each domain looks like, but is not confident about the relative arrangement.
  • For Multimers: When predicting multi-chain complexes, the PAE heatmap is split by chain. The inter-chain regions (the off-diagonal blocks representing the interaction between chains) are critical. Low PAE scores in these regions provide evidence that the predicted protein-protein interaction is highly confident.

A detailed description of PAE is available in the EBI AlphaFold2 guide (opens in a new tab).

What is an MSA?

MSA (Multiple Sequence Alignment) is a collection of related protein sequences aligned to your query sequence. During evolution, amino acids that touch in 3D space tend to mutate together to maintain the protein's shape. Modelling tools scan the MSA for these paired changes, and if two positions consistently mutate together across different species, the tool deduces that they must physically connect in the folded structure (Jumper et al., 2021 (opens in a new tab)).

The Axes

  • X-axis: Represents your target protein's amino acid sequence from start to finish.
  • Y-axis: Shows the number of aligned sequences (the depth or thickness of the MSA) found in global databases. This is often displayed on a logarithmic scale.

How to Interpret the Shape

  • High, flat plateaus (High Coverage): Hundreds or thousands of sequences align with this region. The modelling tool has rich evolutionary data to work with, which usually results in a highly confident, rigid 3D structure (high pLDDT).
  • Deep valleys or dips (Low Coverage): The model found very few related sequences for this specific stretch of amino acids.
  • Zero coverage: A flat line at the bottom means no evolutionary relatives were found for that segment at all.

Low coverage in MSA usually points to naturally flexible/unstructured regions, lab-added artificial sequences, or highly unique evolutionary traits specific to that organism.

The Colours

Each line is one aligned sequence, coloured by its identity to your query from red (low) to blue/purple (high). White marks gaps where a sequence does not align, and the black line shows how many sequences cover each position.

Chainwise ipTM and Chainwise ipSAE

Chainwise ipTM

ipTM is calculated from the PAE by evaluating all cross-chain residue pairs in a complex. This means that the overall ipTM score can obscure the confidence of specific interactions (e.g. antibody-antigen interactions will be dominated by the high-confidence score between heavy and light chains which can obscure the more relevant interaction with the antigen). In the SBP, chainwise ipTM is computed by post-processing the off-diagonal regions of the PAE matrix corresponding to each pair of chains within the complex.

Chainwise ipSAE

When using ipTM, naturally flexible or non-interacting regions (e.g. long disordered tails) can artificially deflate the score of an otherwise high-confidence interface. Interaction prediction Score from Aligned Errors (ipSAE) is a generalisation of AlphaFold's standard ipTM score (Dunbrack, 2025 (opens in a new tab)). ipSAE dynamically filters high-error (high PAE) residue pairs before calculation and normalises the score based on the size of the high-confidence regions. Similar to chainwise ipTM, chainwise ipSAE is computed for each chain pair. The AlphaFoldDB recommends an ipSAE cutoff of 0.6 but true interactions have been found to be enriched as low as ipSAE > 0.3 (Dunbrack, 2025 (opens in a new tab)).

Interpreting Your Results

Below are a few steps you may consider when analysing your results:

  1. Step 1: Did the modelling tool have enough data?

    Before looking at the 3D model, check the foundation of the prediction.

    MSA Coverage: Measures the evolutionary data fed to the modelling tool.

    • 🟢

      Look for: High, flat plateaus (depth > 30 sequences).

    • 🔴

      Watch out for: Deep valleys or zero coverage. The tool is guessing here (usually due to natural disorder, artificial lab tags, or novel sequences).

  2. Step 2: Is the overall shape correct?

    Evaluate the big picture and the global topology.

    3D Structure (Visual): Does it look biologically plausible?

    • 🟢

      Look for: Compact, well-folded domains.

    • 🔴

      Watch out for: Tangled, unstructured "spaghetti" (indicates flexible, disordered regions).

    pTM (Single Chains): Measures global fold confidence.

    • 🟢

      Good: > 0.5 (The general 3D shape is on the right track).

    ipTM (Complexes): Measures global assembly of a multi-chain complex.

    • 🟢

      Good: > 0.8. Note: If this is low, don't throw the model away, as a single floppy chain might be dragging the score down. Move to Step 4.

  3. Step 3: Can I trust the fine details?

    Zoom in to see if the domains and amino acids are accurately placed.

    pLDDT: Measures per-residue confidence (mapped as colours on the 3D viewer).

    • 🟢

      Trust it: Blue (>70). You can trust the backbone and side chains for precise measurements.

    • 🔴

      Ignore it: Orange/Red (<50). Unreliable or naturally flexible.

    PAE Plot: Measures whether different domains are rigidly locked together.

    • 🟢

      Look for: Dark regions (< 5 Å). Dark blocks off the main diagonal mean two separate regions are confidently interacting.

    • 🔴

      Watch out for: Light/white areas. The tool doesn't know how these regions are angled relative to one another.

  4. Step 4: Did the subunits bind correctly? (For Multimers)

    If you are predicting multiple interacting chains, use these metrics to bypass the flaws of the global ipTM score.

    Chainwise ipTM: Breaks the complex down into a pairwise matrix.

    Why use it: It tells you exactly which subunits bind together (e.g., Chains A and B are perfect, but Chain C failed).

    Chainwise ipSAE: The ultimate interface score (Target: > 0.80).

    Why use it: Standard scores unfairly penalise proteins with long, flexible tails. ipSAE mathematically filters out that floppy "noise" to score only the binding interface, giving you the most accurate verdict on the interaction.
