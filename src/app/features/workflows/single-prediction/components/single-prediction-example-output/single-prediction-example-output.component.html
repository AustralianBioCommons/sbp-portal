Understanding Confidence

What is pLDDT?

The predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) is a per-residue measure of local confidence.

The score ranges from 0 to 100. A higher pLDDT means the model has higher confidence in the local structure of that region. Since pLDDT is a per-residue metric, confidence levels often fluctuate across a single protein chain. This means the modelling tool can be very confident in the structure of some parts of the protein and less confident in others.

For more information, refer to this tutorial (opens in a new tab).

How to interpret pLDDT scores
pLDDT score Confidence How to interpret it
> 90 Very high

The local structure is predicted with very high confidence.

Both the backbone and side chains are predicted with high accuracy

70–90 High

The local structure is generally reliable.

A correct backbone prediction with misplacement of some side chains

50–70 Low

Treat the predicted structure with caution.

The model is guessing at the general location, but the structural details are uncertain.

< 50 Very low

Unreliable structure because

  1. Naturally unstructured or flexible "disordered" regions
  2. Insufficient information to predict structure with confidence

What is PAE?

Predicted Aligned Error (PAE) indicates how confident the model is about the relative distance between two specific amino acids within the predicted structure. PAE can be visualised as an easy-to-interpret 2D graph, with protein residues running along the axes. In each square, the shade of green indicates the expected distance error in Ångströms (Å) for a pair of residues. Lower PAE means lower predicted positional error; higher PAE means greater uncertainty.

How to read this plot

  • Dark green diagonal line: This line runs from top-left to bottom-right and represents residues aligned against themselves; confidence is always high by definition, so it is not informative and can be ignored.
  • Darker/low-error regions: The model is highly confident in the relative 3D position of these residues. When a dark block appears off the main diagonal, it means two distant parts of the protein sequence are confidently folded together into a rigid structure (AlphaFold DB FAQ (opens in a new tab)).
  • Lighter/high-error regions: The model is uncertain about their relative position. This often happens when two well-folded domains (which might have great pLDDT scores) are connected by a flexible linker. The modelling tool knows what each domain looks like, but doesn't know how they are angled relative to one another (AlphaFold DB FAQ (opens in a new tab)).
  • For Multimers: When predicting multi-chain complexes, the PAE heatmap is split into quadrants. The inter-chain regions (the off-diagonal blocks representing the interaction between Chain A and Chain B) are critical. Low PAE scores in these regions provide strong evidence that the predicted protein-protein interaction is highly confident (Evans et al., 2021 (opens in a new tab)).

What is an MSA?

MSA (Multiple Sequence Alignment) is a collection of related protein sequences aligned to your query sequence. During evolution, amino acids that touch in 3D space tend to mutate together to maintain the protein's shape. Modelling tools scan the MSA for these paired changes, and if two positions consistently mutate together across different species, the tool deduces that they must physically connect in the folded structure (Jumper et al., 2021 (opens in a new tab)).

The Axes

  • X-axis: Represents your target protein's amino acid sequence from start to finish.
  • Y-axis: Shows the number of aligned sequences (the depth or thickness of the MSA) found in global databases. This is often displayed on a logarithmic scale.

How to Interpret the Shape

  • High, flat plateaus (High Coverage): Hundreds or thousands of sequences align with this region. The modelling tool has rich evolutionary data to work with, which usually results in a highly confident, rigid 3D structure (high pLDDT).
  • Deep valleys or dips (Low Coverage): The model found very few related sequences for this specific stretch of amino acids.
  • Zero coverage: A flat line at the bottom means no evolutionary relatives were found for that segment at all.

Low coverage in MSA usually points to naturally flexible/unstructured regions, lab-added artificial sequences, or highly unique evolutionary traits specific to that organism.

The Colours

Each line is one aligned sequence, coloured by its identity to your query from red (low) to blue/purple (high). White marks gaps where a sequence does not align, and the black line shows how many sequences cover each position.

Chainwise ipTM and Chainwise ipSAE

Chainwise ipSAE

Developed by Roland Dunbrack Jr. at the Fox Chase Cancer Center, interaction prediction Score from Aligned Errors (ipSAE) is a robust alternative to AlphaFold's standard ipTM metric. It corrects a major mathematical limitation, as ipTM calculates binding confidence by averaging the alignment quality of all residues across interacting chains. Consequently, naturally flexible or non-interacting regions, e.g., long disordered tails, artificially deflate the score of an otherwise perfectly predicted interface. For instance, artificially trimming the flexible tails off a kinase sequence boosts its ipTM score, showing the metric is biased by sequence length. ipSAE resolves this by dynamically filtering out high-error (high PAE) residue pairs before calculation, normalizing the score based solely on the high-confidence interacting regions. ipSAE is scored on a scale from 0 to 1, where values near 1.0 indicate a highly confident interface. (Dunbrack, 2025 (opens in a new tab)).

Chainwise ipTM

Chainwise interface Predicted Template Modeling score (Chainwise ipTM) is a more granular, pair-specific version of AlphaFold-Multimer's standard ipTM metric. Instead of giving a single score for an entire multi-protein complex, chainwise ipTM breaks down binding confidence for every specific pair of chains interacting within that complex. AlphaFold’s global ipTM scores the entire complex at once, meaning one poorly docked chain can severely drag down the score and hide perfectly predicted interactions between other chains.

The pipeline calculates chainwise ipTM by extracting pairwise data from the raw PAE matrix, generating a grid that pinpoints the exact binding confidence for every individual chain-to-chain interaction.

Interpreting Your Results

There isn’t one “magic” score; you need to interpret pTM + pLDDT + PAE + Chainwise ipTM + Chainwise ipSAE + MSA coverage together.

Below are a few steps you may consider when analysing your results:

  1. Step 1: Did the modelling tool have enough data?

    Before looking at the 3D model, check the foundation of the prediction.

    MSA Coverage: Measures the evolutionary data fed to the modelling tool.

    • 🟢

      Look for: High, flat plateaus (depth > 30 sequences).

    • 🔴

      Watch out for: Deep valleys or zero coverage. The tool is guessing here (usually due to natural disorder, artificial lab tags, or novel sequences).

  2. Step 2: Is the overall shape correct?

    Evaluate the big picture and the global topology.

    3D Structure (Visual): Does it look biologically plausible?

    • 🟢

      Look for: Compact, well-folded domains.

    • 🔴

      Watch out for: Tangled, unstructured "spaghetti" (indicates flexible, disordered regions).

    pTM (Single Chains): Measures global fold confidence.

    • 🟢

      Good: > 0.5 (The general 3D shape is on the right track).

    iPTM (Complexes): Measures global assembly of a multi-chain complex.

    • 🟢

      Good: > 0.8. Note: If this is low, don't throw the model away, as a single floppy chain might be dragging the score down. Move to Step 4.

  3. Step 3: Can I trust the fine details?

    Zoom in to see if the domains and amino acids are accurately placed.

    pLDDT: Measures per-residue confidence (mapped as colors on the 3D viewer).

    • 🟢

      Trust it: Blue (>70). You can trust the backbone and side chains for precise measurements.

    • 🔴

      Ignore it: Orange/Red (<50). Unreliable or naturally flexible.

    PAE Plot: Measures whether different domains are rigidly locked together.

    • 🟢

      Look for: Dark regions (< 5 Å). Dark blocks off the main diagonal mean two separate regions are confidently interacting.

    • 🔴

      Watch out for: Light/white areas. The tool doesn't know how these regions are angled relative to one another.

  4. Step 4: Did the subunits bind correctly? (For Multimers)

    If you are predicting multiple interacting chains, use these metrics to bypass the flaws of the global iPTM score.

    Chainwise ipTM: Breaks the complex down into a pairwise matrix.

    Why use it: It tells you exactly which subunits bind together (e.g., Chains A and B are perfect, but Chain C failed).

    Chainwise ipSAE: The ultimate interface score (Target: > 0.80).

    Why use it: Standard scores unfairly penalize proteins with long, flexible tails. ipSAE mathematically filters out that floppy "noise" to score only the binding interface, giving you the most accurate verdict on the interaction.

Note: When analyzing multimeric complexes, prioritise the iPTM score over pTM. Because the overall accuracy of a complex depends on the precise orientation of its subunits, a high iPTM (confident interface) strongly implies a high-quality global prediction for the entire complex (Reference: EBI training material (opens in a new tab)).
